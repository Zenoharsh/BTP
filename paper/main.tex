\begin{abstract}
Vision-language models (VLMs) can read documents and charts and reason about scenes, but they are large,
and serving several tasks on one small device usually means storing several models. We adapt
Qwen2-VL-2B, quantised to 4 bits, to document VQA, chart QA and visual spatial reasoning with a mixture
of LoRA experts (MoE-LoRA) whose router reads the question once and picks one expert for the whole
answer. Training runs on a single 16\,GB T4 GPU, with the full-precision model as a distillation teacher.
On held-out test data this sequence-level MoE-LoRA is as accurate as a dense LoRA of the same rank
(\textminus0.7 points, 95\% CI [\textminus1.7, +0.4], three seeds), yet its experts line up with the
tasks without ever seeing a task label, carrying about ten times more task information than
token-level experts. Since every request uses a single expert, the experts fold into ordinary per-task
checkpoints with no loss: on a 4\,GB GTX~1650 laptop GPU the folded model costs exactly what the 4-bit
base costs (2.0\,GB peak, 18.8 tokens/s), while keeping the router adds 11\% memory and slows decoding
by 24\%. With HQQ 4-bit quantisation, MoE-LoRA reaches 79.6 points, 2.8 above the original
full-precision model (95\% CI [+0.4, +5.2]), while peak GPU memory drops from 4.2\,GB to 1.9\,GB; the
full-precision model does not even fit on the 4\,GB GPU. We also tried MoPEQ-style per-layer bit
allocation. It helps the untuned model a little at every memory budget (+1.2 to +2.2 points, not
significant on its own), stops helping once the adapters are trained, and goes badly wrong when
sensitivity comes from a cheap Hessian-trace estimate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Compact VLMs such as Qwen2-VL-2B~\cite{wang2024qwen2vl} can read documents and charts and reason about
scenes, so they are natural candidates for offline use on laptops, embedded boards and field equipment.
Two things get in the way. The full-precision weights alone take about 4.1\,GB, and many edge GPUs have
4\,GB or less. And a single device is often asked to do several different jobs: one shared model tends
to lose accuracy, while one model per job multiplies storage.

Low-rank adapters (LoRA)~\cite{hu2022lora} trained on top of a 4-bit base
(QLoRA)~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} make training cheap enough for one small GPU. Mixtures of LoRA
experts~\cite{zadouri2023molora,dou2023loramoe} tackle the multi-task problem by giving the model several
adapters and a router, much like sparse mixture-of-experts layers~\cite{shazeer2017moe,fedus2022switch}.
The catch is deployment: a live router and extra experts cost memory and latency, which an edge device
can least afford, and token-level routers rarely end up with experts that match recognisable tasks.

We ask whether MoE-LoRA can be trained with multi-task capacity and then shipped at the cost of the base
model. Concretely, we contribute:
\begin{itemize}
\item A MoE-LoRA layer for the MLP blocks of a 4-bit VLM whose router decides once per request, from the
question text, which expert to use, trained with top-$k$ distillation from the full-precision model on
one T4 GPU.
\item A three-seed comparison on held-out test data against dense LoRA (same rank and same parameter
count) and token-level routing, with paired bootstrap tests.
\item Evidence that the per-request router finds the tasks by itself (0.48 vs 0.05 bits of task--expert
mutual information) and that its experts fold into plain per-task checkpoints, which run on a 4\,GB
laptop GPU with no overhead over the base model.
\item A memory--accuracy study against the unquantised model, covering per-component and MoPEQ-style
per-layer mixed precision: with an HQQ base the adapted model is significantly more accurate than fp16
($+2.8$ points, $p=0.01$) with peak GPU memory cut from 4.2\,GB to 1.9\,GB.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Parameter-efficient adaptation.} LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} learns low-rank updates to frozen
weights; QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qlora} trains them on top of a 4-bit NF4 base, which we adopt. MoPEQ~\cite{chitty2025mopeq}
assigns mixed bit widths to the experts of large pretrained MoE VLMs by sensitivity; our small experts
are folded away (Section~\ref{sec:concl}), so we keep them in full precision and study mixed precision of the base.
\textbf{Mixture of experts.} Sparsely gated MoE layers~\cite{shazeer2017moe} and Switch
Transformers~\cite{fedus2022switch} route tokens to experts with load-balancing losses; router
$z$-losses~\cite{zoph2022stmoe} stabilise training. Mixtures of LoRA experts apply the idea to
adapters~\cite{zadouri2023molora,dou2023loramoe}. These works route at token level and keep the router
at inference. We route at sequence level and fold experts away for deployment. We distil~\cite{hinton2015distilling} from the unquantised model into the 4-bit student.

\subsection{Shared-base MoE-LoRA layer}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} summarises the approach. Each MLP block of the language model, $y=W_d\,(\sigma(W_g x)\odot W_u x)$, keeps its frozen 4-bit
weights and receives $E$ LoRA experts on each of the three projections:
\begin{equation}
W_p' x = W_p x + \sum_{e=1}^{E} g_e(x)\, s\, B_{p,e} A_{p,e}\, x,\quad p\in\{g,u,d\},
\end{equation}
with rank $r$, scale $s=\alpha/r$ and gate vector $g(x)$. Because the frozen projection is computed once and left
unscaled, setting every $B$ to zero gives back the base model exactly. The $E$ adapters of a projection are
stored stacked, and the gate is applied as a block mask on the rank dimension, so the cost equals one
LoRA of rank $Er$ without a loop over experts. For the selected top-$k$ experts the gate is
$g_e=(E/k)\,p_e$, where $p$ is the router's softmax output; a dense LoRA baseline is the special case $E=1$.

\subsection{Token-level vs sequence-level routing}
A token-level router, as in standard MoE, looks at every token's hidden state and may pick a different
expert for each. Our sequence-level router decides once per request. It averages the layer input over
the \emph{text} tokens of the prompt, ignoring image tokens, runs the router on that average, and keeps
the chosen expert for the whole sequence. The choice is made during prefill and reused while decoding,
so the generated answer has no say in it. Because a single decision per
sample makes per-batch load statistics meaningless at batch size 1, the load-balancing loss
$E\sum_e \bar u_e \bar p_e$ uses an exponential moving average $\bar u$ of expert usage (decay 0.99);
a router $z$-loss~\cite{zoph2022stmoe} is added.

\subsection{Training objective}
The loss is $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{CE}+\lambda_{KD}\mathcal{L}_{KD}+\lambda_{lb}\mathcal{L}_{lb}+\lambda_z\mathcal{L}_z$,
computed on answer tokens only. The teacher is the same model in fp16 without quantisation. We cache its
top-50 probabilities at temperature $T=2$ for every answer token. At $T=2$ the top-50 tokens cover only 44\% of
the probability mass on average, so renormalising them would distort the teacher. We instead lump the
remaining mass into one ``tail'' bucket and take $\mathcal{L}_{KD}$ as the exact KL divergence between
teacher and student over these $k{+}1$ buckets, scaled by $T^2$.

\subsection{Folding experts for deployment}
In \emph{fixed} mode the gate is a constant vector $\bar g$ and each projection's update is the dense
matrix $\sum_e \bar g_e s B_e A_e$, which is added to the unquantised base weights and saved as a standard
Qwen2-VL checkpoint. A \emph{task profile} uses, for each layer, the expert that the router chose most often
for that task on the development set, with its mean gate value; a \emph{full merge} uses the expected gate
over all development samples. With sequence-level routing a request uses exactly one expert per layer, so a
task profile reproduces the routed computation whenever the router's choice agrees with the profile.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Data.} 400/50/200 training/development/test samples per task from DocVQA~\cite{mathew2021docvqa}
(document\_ocr), ChartQA~\cite{masry2022chartqa} (chart\_qa) and VSR~\cite{liu2023vsr} (spatial\_reasoning),
1200/150/600 in total. Metrics: ANLS~\cite{biten2019stvqa} for documents, relaxed accuracy for charts and
accuracy on True/False for VSR; we report their macro average. The development set (50 samples per task, where a few
points on one task are a handful of questions) is used only for epoch selection; all comparisons use the
four times larger test set.

\textbf{Model and training.} Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct with the language model in 4-bit NF4 (double
quantisation, fp16 compute). Images are resized by the processor to at most 401{,}408 pixels
(``512\,px''), chosen by a resolution sweep on the base model (dev macro 70.5/75.9/76.5 at
256/512/768\,px). Variants: \emph{dense16} (LoRA rank 16, 14.1M parameters), \emph{dense64} (rank 64,
56.4M, parameter-matched to the MoE), \emph{moe} (4 experts of rank 16, top-1, token-level routing, 56.6M)
and \emph{moe\_seq} (the same with sequence-level routing). AdamW, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$
($10^{-3}$ for routers), cosine schedule with 5\% warm-up, batch 1 with 8-step accumulation, 2 epochs,
gradient checkpointing; $\lambda_{KD}=1$, $\lambda_{lb}=0.01$, $\lambda_z=10^{-3}$. One run takes 64--69
minutes on one T4. We ran seeds 0--2 for dense16 and moe\_seq, 0--1 for moe and seed 0 for
dense64; reruns reproduce scores to the last digit. Significance: paired bootstrap over test samples (stratified by
task, 10{,}000 resamples, per-sample scores averaged over seeds).

\textbf{Hardware.} Training and evaluation: NVIDIA T4 (16\,GB); edge: HP Victus laptop,
GTX~1650 (4\,GB, no tensor cores), Intel i5-12450H, 8\,GB RAM. Greedy decoding, batch 1.

Quantising to 4-bit cuts weight memory from 4213\,MiB to 1390\,MiB but costs 5.2 points. The adapters
recover this loss: at 512\,px dense16 (78.4) and moe\_seq (77.7) score on par with or above the

mostly from spatial reasoning (+7 to +9 points), where fine-tuning teaches the yes/no answer format;
document OCR stays slightly below the unquantised model.

Halving the image budget is harder. At 256\,px document OCR falls by about 10 points for every model,
since small text becomes unreadable, while spatial reasoning is unaffected. Sequence-level MoE-LoRA

memory, which decides whether a model fits the 4\,GB GTX~1650.

\subsection{Where the 4-bit loss comes from}
Table~\ref{tab:vis} keeps one component of the base in fp16 and quantises the rest to NF4 (test, 512\,px).
Both components contribute: an fp16 vision encoder recovers 1.7 of the 5.2 points lost to 4-bit
quantisation for 949\,MiB, an fp16 language model 3.2 points for 1875\,MiB, about the same gain per
megabyte. The vision encoder matters most for document and chart reading, the language model for
spatial reasoning. After fine-tuning, however, the difference almost vanishes: with an fp16 vision
encoder dense16 reaches 78.6 (vs 78.4 with a 4-bit encoder) and moe\_seq 78.0 (vs 77.7), for
about 1\,GB more memory. Trained on the quantised encoder with distillation from the fp16 model,
the adapters absorb the quantisation error, so a uniform 4-bit base is the better edge trade-off.

\subsection{Sensitivity-guided mixed precision}
\label{sec:mixed}
Following MoPEQ~\cite{chitty2025mopeq}, we split the frozen base into 89 units (32 vision blocks, the
vision--language merger, and the attention and MLP of each of the 28 language layers; 1.97B
parameters) and choose 2, 3, 4 or 8 bits per unit with HQQ~\cite{badri2023hqq} (group size 64).
Sensitivity is measured in two ways on 32 training samples: directly, as the KL divergence between the
fp16 model and the model with only that unit quantised, and with the HAWQ/MoPEQ
proxy~\cite{dong2019hawq}, the Hessian trace per parameter (Hutchinson estimate with finite-difference
Hessian-vector products, 16 samples at 256\,px, the most a T4 allows) times the quantisation error.
An exact knapsack then minimises the summed sensitivity at the memory of uniform 3, 3.5 or 4 bits.

Before fine-tuning (Table~\ref{tab:mopeq}), KL-guided allocation beats uniform quantisation at every
budget (+1.2 points at 4 bits, +2.2 at 3; $p=0.13$ and $0.09$, so not individually significant on 600
samples) and matches uniform 4-bit with 11\% less quantised memory at 3.5 bits. The allocator protects the vision--language merger, the
first and last language MLPs and the first vision block, and spends more bits on the vision encoder
than on the language model; spatial reasoning gains most (+6 points at the 4-bit budget).

The Hessian proxy fails: its plans lose 15--40 points against uniform quantisation, because it rates
several units as nearly insensitive and sends them to 2 bits, where the true damage (by KL) is 10--50
times that at 3 bits. Its ranking correlates weakly with the measured KL (Spearman $\rho=0.17$--$0.27$),
and 26 of the 89 trace estimates are not even positive (Fig.~\ref{fig:klhess}); clamped to zero, these
units look free to quantise. Fig.~\ref{fig:mopeqbits} shows the resulting plans side by side.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{figs/kl_vs_hessian.pdf}
\caption{Hessian-trace versus measured KL sensitivity of the 89 units at 3 bits. Units whose trace
estimate is $\le 0$ are drawn at the floor of the plot.}
\label{fig:klhess}
\end{figure}

We blame the noisy, low-budget estimate and the second-order approximation at 2--3 bits, not
Hessian-aware allocation itself; on edge-scale compute a 20-minute direct KL probe is more reliable.

After fine-tuning, moe\_seq on uniform HQQ 4-bit reaches 79.6\,$\pm$\,0.8,
significantly above the full-precision model ($p=0.01$) with 2.4$\times$ less weight memory (1724 vs
4213\,MiB; peak 1909 vs 4325\,MiB), and 1.9 points above the same recipe on NF4 (95\% CI $[+0.0,+3.8]$, $p=0.02$). On the KL-guided 3.5-bit base it reaches
78.0, saving 94\,MiB but losing 1.6 points: as with the fp16 vision encoder above, the adapters absorb
most of the quantisation error, so a better uniform quantiser helps more than a cleverer allocation.
HQQ dequantisation in PyTorch is about twice as slow as NF4 on the T4 (1.0 vs 0.56\,s per answer), so
NF4 remains our latency reference.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}
Our sequence-level MoE-LoRA, trained on one T4 GPU, achieves on held-out test data:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \emph{Better than full precision at less than half the memory:} on an HQQ 4-bit base, MoE-LoRA
scores 79.6 vs 76.8 for the original fp16 model ($+2.8$, $p=0.01$), with peak GPU memory cut from
4.2\,GB to 1.9\,GB; HQQ itself adds 1.9 points over NF4 ($p=0.02$). Only ours fits a 4\,GB GPU.
\item \emph{Quantisation loss recovered:} adapters lift the 4-bit base from 71.6 to 77.7--79.6, and
spatial reasoning from 60.5 to 75.8--78.2.
\item \emph{Experts that find the tasks on their own:} sequence-level routing carries 0.48 bits of task
information against 0.05 for token-level routing, with no task labels, at the accuracy of dense LoRA.
\item \emph{Zero deployment overhead:} folded checkpoints lose 0.2 points and run on the GTX~1650 at
exactly the base model's cost (2054\,MiB, 18.8 tokens/s); keeping the router would cost 11\% more
memory and 24\% slower decoding. fp32 compute cuts its time to first token from 12.8 to 3.0\,s.
\item \emph{Fewer image tokens:} at 256\,px (45\% fewer tokens, about 35\% shorter training) MoE-LoRA
stays within noise of the fp16 model and 1.2 points ahead of dense LoRA.
\item \emph{Where precision matters:} the language model causes 3.2 and the vision encoder 1.7 of the
5.2 points lost to 4-bit; KL-guided per-layer bits help the untuned model by 1.2--2.2 points, a cheap
Hessian estimate fails, and after fine-tuning a good uniform quantiser is the best choice.
\end{itemize}
Limitations: three tasks of 400 training samples, one 2B backbone, task profiles that assume the task is
known, and HQQ being slower than NF4 in PyTorch. Expert-level mixed precision as in MoPEQ gives little here,
because our experts are 2.3\% of the parameters and are folded away. We expect it to matter when experts
are a large share of the weights: pretrained MoE VLMs, larger experts that must stay unfolded, or
10B-scale backbones where a few gigabytes decide whether a model fits on an edge device.
